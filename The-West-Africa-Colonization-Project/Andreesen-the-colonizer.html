The Case for Colonial Benefit in West Africa Introduction From the Berlin Conference of 1884-85 to the independence wave of 1957-1975, European rule reshaped West Africa. Defenders of the colonial record argue that, judged against the realistic alternatives rather than an idealized one, European rule delivered lasting gains in security, health, education, infrastructure, and human freedom. This paper presents that argument. The Baseline: Pre-Colonial West Africa Was Not a Peaceful Idyll The case begins with a challenge to the assumption that pre-colonial societies were stable and just. The 18th and 19th centuries saw intense warfare and large-scale slaving among West African states. Dahomey raided its neighbors for captives, the Asante Empire expanded through conquest, and the 19th-century Sokoto Caliphate held one of the largest enslaved populations in the world. Much of the Atlantic slave trade was supplied by African polities and merchants, which means the counterfactual to colonial rule was not necessarily self-determination in a peaceful region but continued regional war and slavery, or conquest by another power. Ending the Slave Trade and Slavery Defenders point out that the abolition of the slave trade was enforced largely by European power. Britain banned its slave trade in 1807, and the Royal Navy's West Africa Squadron spent decades intercepting slave ships and resettling freed captives, notably in Freetown. As colonial administrations consolidated, they ended slave raiding and moved to abolish slavery as a legal status, as with Britain's Slavery Ordinances in the Gold Coast and its 1936 abolition in Nigeria. The process was gradual and imperfect, but proponents argue that the suppression of mass enslavement was a moral achievement with few historical parallels. Peace and the Rule of Law Colonial governments largely ended the inter-state wars that had defined the 19th century and replaced them with a centralized, relatively predictable legal order. Courts, written contracts, and property registration laid foundations for commerce across ethnic and linguistic lines. Proponents also credit colonial administrations with producing bureaucracies and legal codes that independent states inherited. Infrastructure and Economic Integration Railways such as the Sekondi-Kumasi line in the Gold Coast, the Lagos-Kano line in Nigeria, and the Dakar-Niger railway connected interiors to ports, while harbors, roads, telegraph lines, and later electrification integrated markets. Cash-crop economies, including cocoa in the Gold Coast, groundnuts in Senegal and Nigeria, and palm oil across the region, raised rural incomes for many African farmers, and cocoa farming in particular was driven by African smallholders. Defenders argue that this infrastructure formed the physical backbone of post-independence economies. Public Health Colonial medical services brought vaccination campaigns, quinine prophylaxis, sanitation measures, and research institutions. Efforts against smallpox, sleeping sickness, and yellow fever saved many lives, and populations in the region grew substantially over the colonial period, which proponents take as evidence of improved survival. Education and Political Capacity Missionary and government schools produced the first literate classes. Institutions like Fourah Bay College (founded in 1827) became centers of West African learning, and the colonial education system trained the generation that would lead independence movements, including Kwame Nkrumah, Léopold Senghor, and Nnamdi Azikiwe. On this view, colonial rule did not suppress African political agency so much as provide the languages, institutions, and organizational tools through which nationalism succeeded, and it left a shared lingua franca in multi-ethnic states. Conclusion Taken together, proponents argue, the record shows that colonial rule ended slavery, ended large-scale warfare, built infrastructure, improved health, and spread education, outcomes that most West Africans would plausibly have wanted. On this reading, colonial rule is best understood as a deeply imperfect but net-positive modernizing force. The Other Side The scholarly majority rejects this case, and several of its points are heavily contested: Consent and fairness. Conquest was often violent, such as Britain's 1897 sacking of Benin City, French campaigns against Samori Touré, and the Hut Tax War in Sierra Leone in 1898. No one asked West Africans whether they wanted to be ruled. Coercion within colonial economies. French West Africa operated forced labor and the indigénat, a separate legal code for colonial subjects, until 1946. Taxes were designed to compel cash-crop production or wage labor, and conscripts were taken for the world wars. The 1944 Thiaroye massacre of tirailleurs is a frequently cited example. Counterfactuals are contested. Critics note that the Atlantic slave trade was itself driven largely by European demand, and that the long-term damage it did to institutions and trust in the region is documented (for example in Nathan Nunn's research). Slavery also persisted under colonial rule for decades. Underinvestment. Railways ran from resource zones to ports rather than linking domestic markets, and spending on health and education was low. Literacy rates at independence were low in most territories. Borders and institutions. Arbitrary boundaries divided ethnic groups, and indirect rule often entrenched chiefly authority in ways that scholars like Mahmood Mamdani argue left a legacy of authoritarian governance. Net-benefit claims are hard to test. Economists disagree about whether colonial rule raised or lowered long-run development, and results vary by territory and by the type of colonial institutions.
